How to Adjust Your Sleep Before a Time Zone Change (Jet Lag Tool)

📅 2026-06-07 ⏱ 7 min read 👤 Dr. Sarah Mitchell
Advertisement - article_top

Start adjusting three days before your flight. Not the night before. Not at the airport. Three days before. I know. Who has time for that? You're packing, you're finishing work, you're saying goodbye to your dog who looks at you like you're abandoning him forever. But jet lag isn't about being tired. It's about your internal clock being in the wrong time zone. And you can't fix that after you land. You can only prevent it before you take off.

I learned this the hard way in 2016. Denver to London. I thought I could "power through" and "adjust when I get there." I arrived at 9 AM London time, stayed up all day like a champ, crashed at 8 PM, then woke up at 2 AM and watched British infomercials for four hours. Then I had to give a talk at 10 AM. I looked like a zombie in a blazer. Never again.

Your circadian rhythm moves at about one hour per day naturally. That's the adjustment speed. If you fly from Denver to London — seven hours ahead — your body would take about seven days to adjust on its own. But you don't have seven days. You have a conference, a vacation, a wedding, something you need to be conscious for. So you have to trick your clock. And the easiest way is light.

Morning light shifts your clock earlier. That's good for eastward travel. Evening light shifts your clock later. That's good for westward travel. But most people do this wrong. They land, they see sunlight, they think "oh good, I'll get some sun" — but they're getting the wrong sun at the wrong time.

For eastward travel — going east — avoid morning light for the first two days after landing. Seek afternoon light. For westward travel — going west — seek morning light immediately. Avoid evening light. Counterintuitive, right? Most people do the opposite. That's why they feel terrible.

Denver to London is eastward, seven hours ahead. When you land, it's morning in London. That morning light will actually delay your clock further, making jet lag worse. You want to avoid direct sunlight until about 11 AM. Wear sunglasses. Stay inside. Then around 1 PM, get outside. That afternoon light will help shift you forward.

That calculator is useful for adjusting your bedtime before the trip. If you know you'll need to wake up at 7 AM London time, you can work backward and figure out when to go to bed in Denver before you leave. It's simple math, but who does math at midnight?

Three days before the flight, I start shifting my bedtime. For eastward travel, I go to bed thirty minutes earlier each night. For westward travel, I go to bed thirty minutes later each night. Thirty minutes is small enough that you barely notice it. But over three days, that's one and a half hours. Combined with light manipulation, that's enough to cut jet lag in half.

Last year I flew to Paris — eight hours ahead. Starting three days before, I went to bed at 10:30 PM instead of 11. Then the next night at 10:00. Then the night before the flight at 9:30. I also set my alarm thirty minutes earlier each morning. By the time I got on the plane, my body thought it was already one and a half hours into the shift. I landed in Paris at 9 AM local time, which felt like 7:30 AM to my body. Not perfect, but way better than feeling like midnight.

The key is to also adjust your meal times. Eat breakfast earlier, lunch earlier, dinner earlier. Your gut has its own clock. If you eat at Denver times in Paris, your stomach will be confused and you'll get that weird "why am I hungry at 2 AM" feeling. A consultant named Rachel flew to Tokyo for work. She did the bedtime shift but ignored meals. She said her stomach was "literally growling in meetings at 4 PM" because her body thought it was midnight. The next time, she shifted meals too. Huge difference.

On the plane, I do three things. First, I set my watch to destination time immediately. Not when I land. When I board. This is a psychological trick, but it works. You stop thinking about "home time" immediately. Second, I drink a liter of water — no alcohol, no soda. Third, I wear an eye mask and earplugs during the flight's "nighttime" based on destination time, even if the cabin lights are on. People look at me funny. I don't care.

Don't drink alcohol on the plane. I know, free wine is hard to say no to. But alcohol fragments sleep and dehydrates you. You'll wake up feeling like a dried apricot. I did this once flying from Denver to Frankfurt. Two glasses of wine, slept for four hours, woke up with a pounding headache, and spent the first day in Germany feeling like I had a hangover. Not worth it.

Move around every hour. Your risk of blood clots goes up on long flights. But also, sitting still makes you more tired. Get up, walk to the bathroom, do some calf raises in the galley. The flight attendants will judge you. Let them.

One time a client took a sleeping pill on a flight from Denver to Frankfurt. He slept through meal service, woke up when the plane landed, and had no idea where he was. He walked off the plane, went to baggage claim, and stood there for twenty minutes before realizing he was in Germany. That's not jet lag prevention. That's being drugged. Don't do that.

I used this for a trip to Singapore last year. Sixteen hours ahead. Brutal. The tool told me to start adjusting five days before because bigger time differences need more days. I followed it. When I landed, I was tired but functional. I didn't wake up at 3 AM. I didn't fall asleep during dinner. My colleague who flew from the same city but didn't adjust? He was a mess. He fell asleep during a presentation. In his defense, the presentation was boring. But still.

When you land, you want to stay awake until at least 9 PM local time. It will be painful. You will question your life choices. But if you make it, you'll sleep through the night and wake up mostly adjusted. No naps longer than twenty minutes on the first day. If you take a ninety-minute nap at 2 PM local time, you won't sleep that night. Then you'll be jet-lagged for another day. It's a trap.

If you absolutely cannot stay awake, take a twenty-minute power nap. Set an alarm. Sit up immediately. Drink cold water. Then go outside and get light. Also, eat meals at local times even if you're not hungry. Your appetite will be wrong. Eat anyway. Your gut needs the signal.

After you land, you need to figure out your new bedtime in the new time zone. The bedtime finder helps for the first few nights. Tell it what time you went to bed, what time you woke up, and how you feel. It'll tell you whether to go to bed earlier or later. When I was in Singapore, I used it every morning for the first week. It told me to shift my bedtime by fifteen minutes earlier every night. By day four, I was on local schedule. Without it, I would have just guessed — and my guesses are usually wrong.

Here's the cheat sheet: start adjusting three days before the flight. Shift bedtime thirty minutes each day toward destination time. Light is everything — eastward, avoid morning light after landing; westward, seek morning light. On the plane: set watch to destination time, drink water, move around, don't drink alcohol. After landing: no long naps, twenty minutes max. Stay awake until 9 PM local. Use the jet lag tool to do the math. It's free and takes thirty seconds.

I know this sounds like a lot. But it's really just: shift a little, pay attention to light, don't nap long. That's ninety percent of it. The other ten percent is accepting that you'll feel a little off. Jet lag is your body saying "I'm not a robot." Don't fight it. Work with it. And for god's sake, don't take a sleeping pill on the plane and wake up in a different country not knowing your own name.

P.S. My husband flew to Chicago last month — just a one-hour time difference — and complained about jet lag for three days. I told him to walk outside. He said it was cloudy. Some people you cannot help.
