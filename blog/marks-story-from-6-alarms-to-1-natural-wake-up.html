Mark's Story: From 6 Alarms to 1 Natural Wake-Up

📅 2026-05-30 ⏱ 6 min read 👤 Dr. Sarah Mitchell
Advertisement - article_top

"I set six alarms. Every single morning."

That's what Mark told me in his first session. Six alarms. He wasn't exaggerating. He showed me his phone. The first one at 6:00 AM. Then 6:09. Then 6:15. Then 6:22. Then 6:30. Then a final "oh shit" alarm at 6:45. He said his wife had started sleeping in the guest room because the noise drove her crazy.

Mark was thirty-four, an accountant in Denver, father of two kids under five. He came to me because he was exhausted all the time and his doctor had run every test — thyroid, iron, vitamin D, sleep apnea — and found nothing. The doctor said "maybe you just need better sleep hygiene." That's doctor-speak for "I don't know what's wrong but here's a vague suggestion."

I asked Mark how he felt when he woke up. He said "like I've been hit by a truck." I asked how long he'd been using six alarms. He said "about four years, ever since my first kid was born." I asked if he'd ever tried waking up with just one alarm. He laughed. Not a happy laugh. A bitter one.

Here's what was actually happening. Mark was waking up in the middle of deep sleep every single morning. His six alarms weren't helping him wake up — they were fragmenting his last sleep cycle into tiny, useless pieces. He'd fall back asleep after each alarm, start a new cycle, then get yanked out of it seven to nine minutes later. By the time he actually got out of bed, his brain had been through five or six aborted cycles. No wonder he felt like roadkill.

I asked Mark to do a simple experiment. For three mornings, he would set only one alarm. But not at 6:00 AM. He would use the sleep cycle calculator to find a wake-up time that aligned with the end of a ninety-minute cycle. He went to bed around 10:30 PM most nights, so I had him calculate backwards.

The calculator gave him several options. If he went to bed at 10:30 PM, his cycles would end at roughly 12:00 AM, 1:30 AM, 3:00 AM, 4:30 AM, 6:00 AM, and 7:30 AM. So his ideal wake-up times were 6:00 AM or 7:30 AM. He had to be at work by 8:00, so 7:30 was too late. That left 6:00 AM.

Mark was currently setting his first alarm at 6:00 AM, but then hitting snooze until 6:45. So his body was waking up at the right time — 6:00 — but then he was ignoring it and going back into a fragmented mess. I told him: when that 6:00 AM alarm goes off, sit up immediately. Don't hit snooze. Don't lie there thinking about how tired you are. Just sit up. Put your feet on the floor. Stand up. Go to the bathroom. Turn on the light.

He tried it for one morning and texted me at 6:30 AM: "I feel weird. Not bad. Just… different." I asked him to keep going for a week.

Day two, he said he woke up before the alarm. His body had started anticipating the 6:00 wake-up. That's normal. Your circadian rhythm can learn. He sat up, stood up, and made coffee. He said he felt "mostly fine."

Day three, he forgot to set the alarm. He woke up naturally at 5:58 AM. He laughed. He said "my body is smarter than my phone."

By day seven, Mark was using zero alarms. Zero. He was waking up naturally between 5:55 and 6:05 every morning, feeling groggy for about five minutes, then fine. His wife moved back into the bedroom.

But here's the thing. Mark had been using six alarms for four years. Four years of fragmented, horrible mornings. And the fix was just… stopping. Not buying anything. Not meditating. Not drinking kale smoothies. Just not hitting snooze and aligning his wake-up with his natural cycle.

I asked him why he thought he needed six alarms. He said "I was afraid I wouldn't wake up." But he had never actually overslept. The fear was irrational. He just needed permission to trust his body.

Now, Mark's story has a happy ending, but it's not that simple for everyone. Some people genuinely need multiple alarms because they have deep sleep inertia or a medical condition. But for most people, the multiple-alarm habit is exactly that — a habit. One that makes mornings worse.

After Mark fixed his wake-up, we worked on his bedtime. He was still going to bed at random times — sometimes 10:00, sometimes 11:30. That inconsistency was messing with his circadian rhythm. Even though he was waking up well, he wasn't falling asleep easily.

Mark logged his bedtimes and morning feelings for a week. The tool identified that his best sleep happened when he went to bed between 10:15 and 10:45 PM. That became his target window. He started setting a "wind down" alarm at 9:45 PM — that meant no more screens, start brushing teeth, get ready. Then a "bedtime" alarm at 10:15. He stuck to that for two weeks.

His sleep quality score went from fifty-two to seventy-one. He said "I didn't know I could feel this good on seven hours of sleep." He was sleeping seven hours — not eight, not nine. But because it was aligned with his cycles and consistent, it felt better than the eight hours of fragmented sleep he'd been getting.

Mark also stopped drinking coffee after 1 PM. That was hard for him — he loved an afternoon latte. But he switched to decaf or herbal tea. He said his afternoon crash disappeared. Because the crash wasn't from tiredness. It was from caffeine withdrawal. When he stopped the afternoon caffeine, his body leveled out.

I asked Mark to take the full sleep quality quiz at the end of our work together.

He scored seventy-eight. Up from fifty-two. That's a twenty-six-point improvement. Not because he bought a new mattress or a fancy tracker. Because he stopped hitting snooze, found his bedtime window, and cut afternoon caffeine.

Mark still has two kids under five. He still gets woken up sometimes. He still has tired days. But he doesn't use six alarms anymore. He doesn't need them. He told me his wife said "I like you again." That's the best compliment he's ever gotten.

If you're a multiple-alarm person, here's my challenge. Tomorrow morning, set one alarm. One. At the time you actually need to wake up. Not thirty minutes early. Not with snooze options. Just one. When it goes off, sit up, put your feet on the floor, stand up. Do that for three days. See if you feel worse or better. I've worked with dozens of people on this. Most feel better by day two. The ones who feel worse usually have a misaligned wake-up time — they're waking up in the middle of deep sleep. For them, use the cycle calculator to find a better wake-up.

You don't need six alarms. Your body is not your enemy. It wants to wake up at the right time. You just have to stop fighting it.

P.S. Mark sent me a photo of his phone's alarm screen about a month after we finished. It had one alarm. Set for 6:00 AM. He said "I keep it just in case, but I haven't needed it in two weeks." That's trust.
