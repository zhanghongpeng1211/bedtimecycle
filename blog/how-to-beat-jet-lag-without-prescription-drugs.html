How to Beat Jet Lag Without Prescription Drugs

📅 2026-05-24 ⏱ 7 min read 👤 Dr. Sarah Mitchell
Advertisement - article_top

Landing at London Heathrow at 9 AM. Your body thinks it's 2 AM. Here's what you do before you even get off the plane.

I've made every jet lag mistake you can make. I've taken sleeping pills and woken up in a different country not knowing my own name. I've drunk wine on the plane and felt like a raisin for three days. I've tried to "power through" and ended up crying in a hotel lobby because I couldn't figure out how to work the coffee machine. Jet lag makes you stupid. Not metaphorically stupid. Actually stupid.

But I've also figured out what works. And most of it is free.

The key is to start before you fly. Not the night before. Three days before. I wrote about this in a previous post, but let me give you a real example. A woman named Hannah — she's a consultant who flies from Chicago to London every month — used to get destroyed by jet lag. She'd arrive on Sunday, feel terrible Monday, sort of okay Tuesday, and then fly back Wednesday. So she was jet-lagged for half her trip. She came to me because she was about to quit her job.

I gave her a plan. Three days before her flight, she shifted her bedtime thirty minutes earlier each night. She also shifted her meals — breakfast, lunch, dinner — thirty minutes earlier. And she wore sunglasses for the first hour after waking up — to delay her light exposure. By the time she boarded the plane, her body was already one and a half hours into the time zone shift. She arrived in London at 9 AM local time, which felt like 7:30 AM to her body. Not perfect, but way better than 2 AM.

On the plane, she did three things. First, she set her watch to London time immediately. Not when she landed. When she boarded. This is a psychological trick, but it works. Second, she drank a liter of water — no alcohol, no soda. Third, she wore an eye mask and earplugs during the flight's "nighttime" — based on London time — even if the cabin lights were on. She said people looked at her funny. She didn't care.

When she landed, she didn't nap. That was the hardest part. She was exhausted. But she stayed awake until 9 PM London time. She got outside in the afternoon light — which helps shift your clock forward. She ate dinner at 7 PM. She went to bed at 9 PM. She slept for nine hours. Woke up at 6 AM feeling fine. She said "I didn't know jet lag could be optional."

Now, not everyone can shift their schedule before a flight. Sometimes you're too busy. Sometimes it's a last-minute trip. That's where the jet lag adjustor comes in.

I landed in Tokyo at 3 PM local time. I was tired but functional. I stayed awake until 10 PM. Slept seven hours. Woke up at 5 AM feeling mostly human. That's as good as it gets for a sixteen-hour shift.

One thing that doesn't work: prescription sleeping pills on the plane. A woman named Rachel took Ambien on a flight from New York to London. She slept through the meal service, woke up when the plane landed, and had no idea where she was. She walked off the plane, went to baggage claim, and stood there for twenty minutes before realizing she was in England. She couldn't remember the flight at all. That's not jet lag prevention. That's sedation.

Another thing that doesn't work: drinking alcohol on the plane to "help you sleep." Alcohol fragments your sleep and dehydrates you. You'll wake up feeling like a dried apricot. I did this once flying from Denver to Frankfurt. I had two glasses of wine, slept for four hours, woke up with a pounding headache, and spent the first day in Germany feeling like I had a hangover. Not worth it.

What does work is strategic napping after you land. But only if you do it right. A twenty-minute power nap can save you. A sixty-minute nap will ruin you. Use the nap planner to time it perfectly.

On the first day after landing, I only allow myself a twenty-minute nap, and only before 2 PM local time. Any nap after 2 PM will steal sleep from that night. And any nap longer than twenty minutes will leave me groggy.

Advertisement - article_middle

I learned this from a guy named Tom, a pilot. Yes, a pilot. He flies long-haul international routes and used to be a mess between trips. He said "I'd land in Singapore, take a three-hour nap, then be wide awake at 2 AM." I told him to cap his naps at twenty minutes. He tried it. He said it was painful for two days, then his body adjusted. Now he lands, takes a twenty-minute nap at the hotel, then goes outside for light. He said his jet lag is "eighty percent better than it used to be."

Here's a trick that sounds fake but works. When you land, eat a meal at the local time even if you're not hungry. Your gut has its own clock. Eating sends a strong signal to your circadian rhythm. If you eat breakfast at 8 AM local time, your body gets the message "we're in a new time zone." If you skip meals or eat at weird times, your gut stays confused.

A woman named Priya flew from San Francisco to Dubai. She didn't eat for the first twelve hours because she "wasn't hungry." She was miserable. The next time, she forced herself to eat at local meal times. Huge difference.

One more thing about light. After you land, get bright light in your eyes at the right time. For eastward travel — going east — avoid morning light for the first two days. Wear sunglasses until 11 AM. Then get afternoon light. For westward travel — going west — get morning light immediately. Go outside within an hour of waking. That light signal is more powerful than coffee.

I know this sounds complicated. That's why I built the jet lag adjustor. You don't need to remember the rules. You just need to follow a plan.

If you can't shift your schedule before the flight, here's a minimal version. On the flight, drink water, don't drink alcohol, set your watch to destination time, and try to sleep if it's nighttime at your destination. When you land, stay awake until at least 8 PM local time. Get light at the right time — use the adjustor to know when. Take a twenty-minute nap if you're dying. Eat meals at local times. You'll be tired for a day, but you won't lose three days.

Jet lag is not inevitable. It's just math. Your body moves about one hour per day naturally. You can speed that up to about one and a half hours per day with light and schedule shifts. But you can't skip it entirely. The people who claim they don't get jet lag? They're either lying or they're already so sleep-deprived that they can't tell the difference.

P.S. Hannah, the consultant I mentioned, sent me a message after her first trip using my plan. She said "I went out for drinks on Monday night. I didn't fall asleep at the table. That's a win." I'll take it.
